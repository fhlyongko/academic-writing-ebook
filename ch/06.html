Chapter 6

Preparing to Write in a New Academic Environment

Chapter purpose. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to apply the central concepts to a real assignment, research paper, thesis, or article in your own discipline.

Learning objectives

  • Analyze audience, genre, and assessment expectations.
  • Establish a sustainable writing environment.
  • Use multilingual resources without weakening authorial responsibility.

Key terms

genrerhetorical purposeassignment briefacademic registermultilingual writer

Read the task before writing

Identify the required genre, purpose, audience, length, evidence, citation style, and submission format. Terms such as discuss, evaluate, compare, and critically analyze signal different intellectual operations. If a prompt remains ambiguous, provide an interpretation and ask a focused question rather than asking the instructor to restate the entire assignment.

Study models strategically

A model text should be analyzed for moves, not copied for sentences. Observe how the writer introduces the problem, positions sources, qualifies claims, organizes evidence, and concludes. Select models from the relevant discipline and genre because conventions differ across laboratory reports, humanities essays, design projects, and social-science theses. Develop a multilingual workflow. Thinking, note-taking, and planning may occur in more than one language. This can expand conceptual resources, but the final argument must remain coherent in English. Define Korean institutional terms when a direct English equivalent is misleading. Keep terminology consistent and avoid literal translation of expressions whose rhetorical force changes across languages.

Create workable conditions

Writing improves through repeated sessions. Set a specific output for each session, remove avoidable distractions, and separate drafting from final editing. Use feedback and tools to diagnose problems, but verify every change. A fluent sentence is not necessarily an accurate sentence.

A multilingual research and writing workflow

International students often search and think across languages. Use this capacity deliberately. Maintain a bilingual term bank containing the Korean term, preferred English rendering, definition, source, and notes on context. Examples include 학위논문 (thesis or dissertation, depending on the degree and institutional usage), 논문계획서 (thesis or research proposal), 예비심사 (preliminary review), 본심사 (final examination or final review), 수정대조표 (revision response table), and 지도교수 (academic advisor, research supervisor, or thesis supervisor). Choose the English term that accurately describes the function; do not assume a one-to-one translation. When citing a Korean source in an English paper, preserve retrievability. Romanize the author's name consistently, translate the title when required by the citation style, identify the original language if useful, and retain the original journal or publisher information accurately. A translation of a quotation is still a quotation: cite the source and indicate that the translation is yours unless an official translation is used. For a paraphrase, verify meaning against the Korean original rather than paraphrasing an automated English translation alone. English names, institutional affiliations, addresses, and degree names should follow official sources. Use the spelling found in the student information system or passport when required; use the university's official English name; and copy the formal English degree title from the university's regulations or diploma documentation. Consistency across the manuscript, submission system, ethics application, and publication metadata prevents indexing and identity problems.

Applications
