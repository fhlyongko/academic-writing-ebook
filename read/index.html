01

Entering Academic Writing in a Korean University

Academic writing is not simply formal English. It is a disciplined way of making knowledge visible to a particular community. A successful text states a purpose, develops claims through reasons and evidence, acknowledges sources, and guides readers through a transparent structure. At a Korean university, international students may encounter conventions that differ from those at their previous schools. The appropriate response is not to abandon prior knowledge, but to learn how expectations are expressed in the present department, course, and assignment.

02

Understanding Research Across Disciplines

Research is a systematic response to a meaningful question. Although the source text emphasizes education, the underlying logic applies across disciplines: define a problem, determine what evidence could answer it, collect and analyze that evidence rigorously, and explain the limits of the resulting claim. Disciplinary differences affect what counts as data, which methods are legitimate, and how findings are reported.

03

Connecting Research and Writing

Writing does more than report a completed study. It reveals gaps in reasoning, forces definitions, and makes the relationship among a problem, evidence, and conclusion inspectable. A researcher who cannot explain why a method answers a question may not yet have achieved adequate alignment. Drafting early therefore improves both the project and the final paper.

04

Planning a Research Project

A research project usually moves through problem identification, preliminary reading, question development, design, ethical review where required, data collection, analysis, drafting, revision, and dissemination. The process is iterative: reading may refine a question, pilot data may change an instrument, and analysis may expose the need for a clearer conceptual definition.

05

Practicing Research and Writing Ethics

Ethics is not a form completed after a design has been finalized. It shapes the research question, recruitment, consent, data handling, analysis, representation, authorship, and dissemination. Researchers should minimize harm, communicate honestly, respect participants, and avoid claims that exceed the evidence.

06

Preparing to Write in a New Academic Environment

Identify the required genre, purpose, audience, length, evidence, citation style, and submission format. Terms such as discuss, evaluate, compare, and critically analyze signal different intellectual operations. If a prompt remains ambiguous, provide an interpretation and ask a focused question rather than asking the instructor to restate the entire assignment.

07

Planning, Drafting, and Revising

Begin with a working question and a provisional answer. Group evidence by the reasons that support that answer, not merely by the order in which sources were read. An outline should display relationships among claims. It may change as the writer discovers new evidence or identifies a stronger structure.

08

Establishing a Research Problem

A topic names an area; a problem identifies a condition that requires explanation or response; a knowledge gap identifies what existing scholarship has not adequately established. A gap is not merely the absence of a study in one location. The writer must explain why the missing knowledge matters and how the proposed inquiry can address it.

09

Building a Conceptual or Theoretical Foundation

A framework identifies concepts and relationships that guide attention. It can shape the research question, definitions, data collection, analysis, and interpretation. A theory should not appear only in the literature review; if it matters to the study, readers should be able to see how it influenced methodological and analytical decisions.

10

Conducting and Writing a Literature Review

A literature review explains what a field knows, how it knows it, where findings converge or conflict, and what remains unresolved. It should establish the intellectual context for the present project. A sequence of article summaries does not accomplish this purpose because it leaves relationships among sources implicit.

11

Writing Methodology as a Coherent Argument

Methods are procedures for generating and analyzing evidence; methodology is the reasoned approach that connects assumptions, research aims, design, and methods. Selecting an interview, survey, or statistical test without explaining this connection reduces methodology to technique.

12

Presenting Findings and Results

The findings or results section should make the answer to each research question easy to locate. Organize by question, hypothesis, analytical category, theme, or a justified sequence. Do not reproduce the chronology of analysis unless that chronology itself matters.

13

Writing the Discussion and Conclusion

The discussion explains what the findings mean, why they may have occurred, how they relate to previous scholarship, and what they contribute. Begin with the principal answer rather than repeating the entire results section. Distinguish explanations supported by evidence from plausible alternatives. Return to the literature and framework. Identify where findings support, extend, complicate, or contradict prior work. Use the theoretical or conceptual framework as an analytical resource, not a vocabulary list. A surprising result is not automatically a contribution; explain its significance and consider methodological or contextual explanations.

14

Reviewing the Complete Manuscript

Confirm that the title, abstract, problem, questions, methodology, findings, discussion, and conclusion describe the same study. Track each research question through the document. Remove claims that lack evidence and evidence that does not serve the argument.

15

Disseminating Research Beyond a Thesis

A thesis is not automatically a journal article, conference presentation, policy brief, or public explanation. Each genre has a different audience, length, selection process, and purpose. Rewriting requires selecting a central contribution and restructuring the material to meet the needs of the new audience.

16

Developing as a Lifelong Academic Writer

Reflection is most useful when it leads to evidence-based action. Review drafts, feedback, research logs, and project outcomes to identify recurring strengths and problems. Ask not only what succeeded, but which conditions and decisions made success possible.
