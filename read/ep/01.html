Entering Academic Writing in a Korean University

Chapter purpose. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to apply the central concepts to a real assignment, research paper, thesis, or article in your own discipline.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Identify the purposes and conventions of academic writing.
  • Distinguish analytical, persuasive, descriptive, and critical functions.
  • Create a personal plan for entering an unfamiliar academic discourse community.

Academic writing as situated communication

Academic writing is not simply formal English. It is a disciplined way of making knowledge visible to a particular community. A successful text states a purpose, develops claims through reasons and evidence, acknowledges sources, and guides readers through a transparent structure. At a Korean university, international students may encounter conventions that differ from those at their previous schools. The appropriate response is not to abandon prior knowledge, but to learn how expectations are expressed in the present department, course, and assignment.

Core qualities

Effective academic prose is clear, precise, evidence-based, ethically sourced, and logically organized. Formality does not require inflated vocabulary or constant passive voice. Writers should select active or passive constructions according to emphasis, define technical terms, quantify claims when the evidence permits, and avoid presenting interpretations as facts. Every paragraph should perform an identifiable function in the larger argument.

Writing functions

A single paper commonly combines several functions. Description establishes what was observed; analysis explains patterns and relationships; persuasion advances a defensible position; and critical writing evaluates assumptions, methods, or implications. International students should identify the function required at each point rather than assuming that all academic writing must sound equally abstract.

Learning local expectations

Assignment sheets, journal articles recommended by the instructor, departmental thesis templates, and professors' feedback are evidence of local conventions. When expectations remain implicit, ask focused questions: What kind of claim is expected? Which citation system applies? How much methodological detail is required? A precise question demonstrates preparation and reduces the risk of misunderstanding.

Korean university context: making expectations explicit

Academic writing is socially situated: readers judge a text against the purposes, evidence practices, and genre conventions of a particular community. International students in Korea therefore need two forms of knowledge simultaneously. They need broadly transferable principles—clear claims, warranted evidence, transparent source use, and coherent organization—and local knowledge about how a department interprets those principles. A course paper, capstone report, laboratory report, thesis proposal, and journal manuscript may all be described as "academic writing." Yet, each assigns different weight to originality, replication, critical synthesis, and practical contribution. At the beginning of an assignment, convert tacit expectations into written questions. Ask what document type is required, who will evaluate it, which language and citation style are expected, whether Korean-language evidence is acceptable, and what counts as an original contribution at the relevant level. At a Korean university, the answer may be distributed across the syllabus, departmental regulations, a graduate school handbook, a laboratory convention, and an instructor's oral guidance. Record the answer and the source of the instruction. When two instructions conflict, seek clarification before drafting, not after submission. Academic English should not require erasing a multilingual identity. Multilingual writers can plan, search, annotate, and test ideas across languages, provided that the final English text explains culturally or institutionally specific concepts and maintains stable terminology. Translanguaging can function as a resource for knowledge construction, but the published argument must remain accessible to its intended readers (Canagarajah, 2013).
