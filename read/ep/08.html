Establishing a Research Problem

Chapter purpose. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to apply the central concepts to a real assignment, research paper, thesis, or article in your own discipline.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Move from a topic to a researchable problem.
  • Establish context, significance, and a knowledge gap.
  • Write aligned purposes and research questions.

KEY TERMS

topicproblemknowledge gappurpose statementresearch questionsignificance

Topic, problem, and gap

A topic names an area; a problem identifies a condition that requires explanation or response; a knowledge gap identifies what existing scholarship has not adequately established. A gap is not merely the absence of a study in one location. The writer must explain why the missing knowledge matters and how the proposed inquiry can address it.

Problem-building sequence

Begin with the broader context, narrow to a specific condition, synthesize what is known, identify a limitation or unresolved issue, and state the consequence of leaving it unresolved. Then state the study's purpose. This sequence allows readers to understand why the project is necessary before encountering detailed questions or methods.

Aligned research questions

Questions should be specific, answerable, ethically appropriate, and consistent with the intended evidence. Avoid combining several studies into one question. Terms such as impact or effect may imply a causal design; use them only when the design can support causal inference. Qualitative questions often emphasize how, what, or in what ways.

Significance without exaggeration

Significance may be conceptual, methodological, practical, or contextual. State the likely contribution at the scale supported by the project. An undergraduate study may clarify a local pattern; a graduate study may refine a concept or provide evidence for a disciplinary debate. Neither should promise to solve a national problem without adequate evidence.

From local concern to researchable problem

A research problem is not established merely because an issue matters. The writer must show what is known, what remains uncertain or contested, why the uncertainty matters to a defined audience, and how the proposed study can produce relevant evidence. Avoid turning personal difficulties into universal claims about all international students or all Korean universities. Use a four-part problem statement: context, established knowledge, bounded gap, and consequence. Then align each research question with a feasible unit of analysis and evidence source. A question about experience may require interviews or reflective records; a performance question may require a defined outcome measure; a question about institutional policy may require document analysis and stakeholder perspectives. Local relevance and wider contribution can coexist. A single Korean university may be a theoretically informative case if the boundaries and selection logic are explicit. The researcher should not claim national representativeness unless the sampling and design support it. State what the study can illuminate, not what it cannot prove.
