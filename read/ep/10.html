Conducting and Writing a Literature Review

Chapter purpose. By the end of this chapter, you should be able to apply the central concepts to a real assignment, research paper, thesis, or article in your own discipline.

LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  • Search, evaluate, organize, and synthesize literature.
  • Differentiate a source list from a literature review.
  • Use citations to construct a scholarly position.

KEY TERMS

search strategyinclusion criteriasynthesisresearch gapcitation chainingliterature matrix

Purpose of the review

A literature review explains what a field knows, how it knows it, where findings converge or conflict, and what remains unresolved. It should establish the intellectual context for the present project. A sequence of article summaries does not accomplish this purpose because it leaves relationships among sources implicit.

Search transparently

Translate the research problem into concepts and search terms, including relevant synonyms. Search appropriate databases, follow reference lists, and examine later works that cite influential studies. Record databases, terms, dates, and inclusion decisions so the search can be explained and updated. Language and database access may shape what becomes visible; acknowledge those limits.

Evaluate and organize

Assess relevance, methodological quality, context, and contribution rather than relying on publication prestige alone. A literature matrix can record purpose, theory, method, sample or materials, findings, limitations, and relevance. Organize the review by themes, debates, methods, chronology, or conceptual relationships, as appropriate to the argument.

Synthesize

Synthesis places multiple sources in relation to one another. Use citations to support a claim about the literature, then explain patterns, contradictions, or limitations. Distinguish the cited authors' conclusions from your evaluation. Conclude by showing how the review leads to the present question and contribution.

Systematic, scoping, and narrative reviews

Choose a review type according to the question. A narrative review can interpret and connect a body of scholarship, but it should still explain the scope and selection. A systematic review uses prespecified, reproducible methods to answer a focused question. A scoping review maps concepts, evidence types, and gaps in a field when it is broad or heterogeneous. PRISMA 2020 provides a reporting framework for systematic reviews, while the PRISMA-ScR extension addresses scoping reviews (Page et al., 2021; Tricco et al., 2018). For Korean and international literature, search across multiple ecosystems when the topic warrants it. RISS International indexes Korean journal articles, theses, books, and serials and supports searches in Korean or English (Korea Education and Research Information Service [KERIS], 2026). Pair it with discipline-appropriate international databases, and record the database, platform, date, complete search string, filters, and result count for each. Search Korean synonyms, romanized variants, and official English institutional terms. A literature review must synthesize rather than inventory. Organize evidence by problem, concept, method, result pattern, or debate—not by a sequence of article summaries. Use a synthesis matrix to compare population, setting, design, evidence, limitations, and relevance. Cite the primary source for a substantive claim whenever possible.
